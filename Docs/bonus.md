## Bonus design task notes

I don't have a specific design to propose since this a bonus, but since it's an interesting question I wrote some notes. (Not required to read!)

### What do people like about RPS?
1. It's fast, simple, and highly dramatic - your fate is determined in a few seconds and mostly by instinct.
2. Bluffing, psyching out your opponent, analyzing them to guess what they'll throw.

I think 1 is a dead end for proposing a new iteration on the game. You can't exactly get simpler and more dramatic than the original.

There's an interesting juxtaposition between 1 and 2. I think there are people who'd claim to be good at RPS because they're good at getting a read on people, and both sides have this element of "instincts", whether that's in a split second choice or reading your opponent.

A RPS game with a high element of bluffing and interpersonal communication leading to a dramatic showdown could work.

#### What's not relevant to RPS?
I think that very tactical thinking, like if it were a RPS roguelike deckbuilder with different power-ups to apply, is not particularly in the spirit of RPS. It's too calculated and not instinct-y enough.

Games that explicitely use rock paper scissors as the basis for something like that, or that use a weapons triangle type thing, are fine and fun, but it doesn't capture the spirit of RPS.

### Multiplayer RPS
You could heighten the communication and bluffing aspect by playing with a group - say, four people in two teams of two. Each team is trying to cooperate amongst themselves without revealing their plans to the other team, so it's an interesting opportunity to lean on shared understanding to give each other hints (or maybe hilariously fail at that) and to try to work out what the other team is up to. It all builds towards a four-person showdown.

I don't have a particular design in mind, it would have to be something where the team has its own goal or restriction they're navigating between each other in addition to each trying to beat an opponent. Maybe it's instant death for both teammates to throw the same thing, making it critically important they communicate with each other, and therefore giving more opportunities for their opponents to figure out their plan?

### Social deduction (hidden role) RPS
Going further on the multiplayer and communication side, RPS would be an interesting combination with a social deduction game. The thing that jumps out at me that makes it not just a mechanic for a run of the mill social deduction game is the idea of the urgency and speed of RPS.

Instead of relatively long rounds where people have to take turns carrying out their secret roles, it would be a fun party game if anyone could call out "Ready! One, two, three, shoot!" and everyone has to stop their current conversations and do RPS battle with someone. In the chaos, players have to carefully observe what the others are doing to try to draw conclusions about their motives, or whatever restrictions or powers (via special handshapes) their hidden roles give them, and argue about who's lying about what while trying to bluff about their throws to accomplish their own goals.

That's far from an actual design, but I can imagine the fun chaos, although there's a risk of people losing track of the special roles and powers, and constantly consulting the rules would drain the energy.

### Bluffing via video game
I was picturing in-person for the above ideas, like traditional RPS, but more relevant to the current task is where bluffing/reading your opponent can come across if it's a video game.

Giving players a lot of expressive power - not necessarily text, but emotes or symbols or other ways of signaling - is fun because it allows them to come up with their own method of communicating using those.

So I can imagine a game of the four-person RPS above where you have a suite of symbols you can send to the others. If you were randomly matched with your partner, will the two of you find a way to get in sync over the multiple rounds of the game? Will meta evolve around different signals, but then how to hide that from your opponent? (Though it might also feel random and annoying.)

The more straightforward idea I had was a limited deck, just like this game, but with 12 cards and you play 11 rounds, or something like that. You can watch what your opponent has left in their deck, but also what patterns they follow (and predict if they're trying to subvert them or not). The communication is in what you play early in the game. Although having emotes to communicate would also be fun and a fun way to try to mislead or confuse your opponent!
